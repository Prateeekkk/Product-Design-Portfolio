Pazy · B2B Fintech

Reimbursements that don't feel like paperwork.

A mobile claim flow that cut submission time in half on bulk filings, introduced mileage reimbursements as a first-class workflow, and stopped employees from asking finance which field is required next.

Role Product Designer
Company Pazy
Year 2023
Surface iOS · Android · Web
01 · Overview

A claim flow built for one receipt, used for thirty.

Pazy is a B2B accounts-payable platform used by finance teams across India. Inside the product, employees file expense reimbursements — fuel, travel, food, software, the long tail of work spend.

The original flow was designed for a person filing one claim. The actual users were sales reps and field staff who'd file thirty receipts on the last day of the cycle and then ping support about why their submission stalled.

02 · The problem

Three failure modes nobody had named.

Talking to ten heavy users over a week, three patterns kept showing up:

  • Repetitive entry. Filing twenty similar claims meant twenty identical sequences. No memory of recent merchants, no carry-over of category, no batch action.
  • Hidden requirements. Fields like GSTIN appeared or disappeared based on category — but the form didn't say why, so users guessed and got bounced back by approvers.
  • Mileage didn't exist. Sales reps were filing "Travel" claims with hand-typed kilometre estimates and screenshots of Google Maps. No one believed the numbers.

Approvers, on the other side, were stuck in a queue of nearly identical claims with no easy way to bulk-action or sort by trust.

"I'm not filing one claim, I'm filing my whole month. Why does it keep asking me the same five questions?"
— Field sales user, week one of research
03 · Role & constraints

What I owned, and what I had to live with.

I led the redesign end-to-end — discovery, IA, interaction, visual, UX writing, and shipping with engineering. I worked alongside a senior designer (Murali), one PM, two iOS engineers, and one Android engineer.

Constraints worth flagging:

  • The approver-side desktop flow couldn't change in the same release — admin panel was downstream of an API rewrite.
  • OCR on receipt images existed but was unreliable on rotated photos. We had to design for the case where the vendor name came back wrong.
  • Some details on the legacy approval logic are under NDA — happy to walk through the approach in a conversation.
04 · Process

From entry form to a chat-style sequence.

The first three rounds of exploration were variations on "make the form less ugly." None of them moved the needle in usability tests. The break came when I stopped treating it as a form at all.

Sales reps were already writing their claims as a sentence in their head — "yesterday, fuel, six hundred bucks, on my way to Pune". The interface fought that mental model. The redesign rebuilt entry as a chat-style sequence: one decision per screen, a persistent running summary at the top, and instant carry-forward of merchant + category between similar claims.

Mileage got its own first-class flow — pick origin and destination on a map, the system computed kilometres at the org's per-km rate, and the receipt became a route image instead of a ticket photo.

05 · Solution

Bulk submission, mileage, smarter form logic.

Three pieces shipped together:

  • Bulk submit. A multi-select on the claims list with a single review screen showing every claim's status in one scrollable card stack. One signature, one tap.
  • Mileage reimbursement. Map-based entry, system- calculated distance, and a confidence indicator on the result so approvers stopped second-guessing it.
  • Conditional fields, transparently. When GSTIN appeared, the form said why ("Required for software / SaaS receipts") and let users skip with a reason. No more silent rejections.

UX writing shifted from neutral form labels to plain instructions. "Add receipt" became "Snap or upload — we'll read the amount." Empty states explained the next action instead of the current state.

06 · Impact

The numbers, and the things you can't number.

~50% faster bulk submission, end-to-end
3.4× jump in mileage claim adoption in the first month
−42% drop in support tickets containing the phrase "this is confusing"

The qualitative win mattered more. Approvers told us they could clear a day's queue in one sitting instead of three. Sales managers stopped flagging reimbursements as a retention concern in 1:1s. And finance stopped getting Slack DMs at 11pm on cycle-end day.

07 · What I'd do differently

Two honest regrets.

One: I shipped without a settings layer for org-specific category rules. Every customer ended up requesting one, and we patched it in two months later. Designing it from day one would have saved a release cycle.

Two: The mileage feature was built around the assumption that the OS map permission would be granted. About 18% of users denied it, and the manual fallback was an afterthought. Today I'd treat the "permission denied" branch as the primary path and the map as the acceleration on top.
