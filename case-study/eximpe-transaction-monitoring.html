Eximpe · Cross-border Payments

Bringing transaction monitoring and reconciliation in-house.

Eximpe was renting these two capabilities from an external payments partner — paying a commission on every transaction and inheriting somebody else's ops UX. An RBI approval unlocked building both surfaces ourselves. I owned the design end-to-end, prototyped in working code with AI, and shipped to engineering.

Role Product Designer · research → design → prototype → ship
Timeline ~1–2 weeks per surface · shipped, pre-adoption
Surface Web · internal Eximpe ops team
01 · Context

AML compliance for a cross-border payments licensee.

Eximpe is RBI-licensed under PA-CB, routing inward remittances from Indian buyers to international merchants. Under PMLA and FEMA, every transaction comes with compliance obligations — including filing STRs with FIU-IND when warranted.

  1. TMS. 27 AML rules + an FRM layer flag transactions for human review. A four-role chain — Compliance Officer → Senior CO → Principal Officer — decides each one.
  2. Reconciliation. Cleared transactions match against the PA-PG partner's settlement file. Mismatches hold for ops.

TMS is the compliance gate before Recon. Most of the design work — and the harder problem — sat in TMS.

02 · Problem

We were renting the most important parts of the stack.

Eximpe ran TMS and Recon through an external payments partner. Two consequences: a commission on every transaction, and an ops UX we couldn't shape — partner's conventions, partner's data model, partner's queues.

An RBI approval unlocked owning the stack. The mandate: build both in-house, migrate ops off the partner, stop paying the commission.

The design problem wasn't "what should this screen look like." It was: the first version had to feel more trustworthy than the tool ops was already using — not just equivalent.
03 · Constraints

Three real constraints.

  • No users to interview. Internal-only product, no live ops team yet. Designed from stakeholder context + industry pattern, not interview transcripts.
  • Heavy NDA scope. Real screens, customer data and partner identifiers stayed internal. The case study explains decisions, not pixels.
  • ~1–2 weeks per surface. Tight cycle: research → design → prototype → handoff. I prototyped in working code (with AI), not static screens.
04 · Approach

Designing without users — and shipping fast anyway.

How do you design for an ops team that doesn't exist yet? Triangulate, instead of waiting:

  • Pattern research with Perplexity. Stripe Radar, Razorpay, public bank-recon UX — read as a literature review, not a copy source.
  • Stakeholder calls instead of user interviews. Ops, finance and engineering had all lived inside the partner dashboard. Three calls gave me the vocabulary and the must-have actions.
  • Prototype as the validation surface. Layout in Figma, working frontend in Claude Code. Stakeholders reviewed in a real browser — exposing empty / loading / "I clicked but nothing happened" states Figma can't show.
  • Ship to the engineering repo, not a Figma link. The prototype became a frontend scaffold engineering extended. Handoff was a PR review.
05 · Solution

Two surfaces, designed to be operated in sequence.

The early product decision I argued for: ship TMS before Recon, not in parallel. Recon can't reconcile what TMS hasn't cleared. Shipping both at once gives ops two new tools and trust in neither. Both surfaces share the same layout, status palette and vocabulary — one model to learn, reused.

1 · Transaction Monitoring (TMS)

First surface in the flow. Every transaction is screened for fraud, risk and policy before it can move downstream. KPI tiles answer where are we today?; the table is the working surface — filterable, selectable, drill-downable.

2 · Reconciliation

Downstream of TMS. Eximpe's record matches against the bank's record line-for-line. Most pairs auto-match into Matched. The operator's day is the other two columns: Needs review (drift, partial captures, timing) and Flagged (stop, decide).

3 · Transaction detail (shared drawer)

Shared by both surfaces. Organised around the four questions an operator asks: what is it · what state · what's happened · what next. One component to learn, one for engineering to maintain.

06 · Iterations

What I tried before this — and why it didn't ship.

The shipped TMS is the fourth or fifth shape this surface took. Most rejected directions look reasonable in isolation — they fail when you sit a Compliance Officer in front of them.

Rejected

Single table for every status.

One filterable table with every status and role-based filters. Broke under use: each role had a different action set, so the row menu became "show buttons your role can click." Replaced with role-scoped tabs (Action Required, Escalation Queue, PO Queue) — each user sees only their own queue.

07 · Edge cases

The job is the failure modes.

Clean transactions need a number, not a surface. The surface is for the ones that don't fit. Five edge cases shaped almost every screen:

  • Amount mismatch. Drift sits on the card itself (amount drift · 0.3%) — operator doesn't open the drawer to find out it's just a fee. Resolution: accept, escalate, investigate.
  • Duplicate transactions. Sibling reference linked inline. Operator confirms, doesn't search.
  • FX failure. Pill names the corridor (Failed · FX), not just "Failed." "Failed" with no reason was the most-cited complaint about the previous tool.
  • Timing drift. Late settlement file → row visibly stuck instead of quietly aging.
  • Manual override. Lives in the drawer with a mandatory reason; timeline records it so the audit trail stays honest.
08 · Impact

Shipped — and honest about where it sits today.

Both surfaces are in production. Migration off the partner is staged, so I don't have defendable adoption numbers yet. I won't fabricate them. What I can defend:

  • The external dependency is replaceable. Once ops migrates, the per-transaction commission goes away and Eximpe owns the ops UX end-to-end.
  • One shared vocabulary across both surfaces. Same palette, drawer and state transitions — no context-switching between TMS and Recon.
  • Design shipped as working code, not screens. Engineering reviewed PRs, not Figma links. Components landed with tokens, states and copy already wired.

Watching post-rollout: time-to-first-recon-completion, auto-resolve rate, and "missing column" requests — the canary for whether the data model is right.

09 · Learnings

Three things this project actually taught me.

  • "No users to interview" is a method problem, not a blocker. Pattern research + stakeholder triangulation + working prototype got me a defendable brief in days. The discipline: name which decisions are still hypotheses so post-rollout you know what to revisit.
  • AI as design medium ≠ AI doing the design. Claude Code compiled my decisions; Perplexity surfaced patterns. The wrong calls still showed up at code-fidelity — just faster than in Figma. Tooling shortens the loop; it doesn't replace judgement.
  • Sequencing was a product decision, not a schedule one. Recon can't reconcile what TMS hasn't verified. Two half-built surfaces ops doesn't trust is worse than one full surface they adopt.
